WWII Tanks

M4 Sherman

The M4 Sherman was an American medium tank that was used by most allied countries during World War II. It was designed in 1941 after the American tanks that were used in the early stages of the war were found to be inadequate against the German tanks. The Sherman was produced at eleven factories across the United States as well as produced in the United Kingdom. From these factories, approximately 50,000 of these tanks were produced. It was armed with a 75mm main gun plus two machine guns: one mounted on the turret and one mounted next to the driver. The tank was also equipped with a large engine, which allowed it to travel at speeds of up to 30 miles per hour and a range of between 100 and 150 miles depending on the variant. The M4 Sherman was a reliable tank, however one downside it faced was that it was not as heavily armored as the German tanks. Because of this, many of the crews would add additional armor to the sides of the tank; the armor would include sandbags, track links, trees, and sometimes even concrete. Unsurprisingly, these added a considerable amount of weight but only provided minimal protection. Despite this, the M4 Sherman was still considered an extremely effective tank. These issues eventually led to the development of many variants of the Sherman, including several that were armed with more powerful guns. Finally, near the end of the war, the M4 Sherman was replaced by the M26 Pershing, which solved the issue of armor and introduced a 90mm gun. Overall, even with its many flaws, the M4 Sherman is still considered one of the most iconic tanks of World War II.

Panzer

The Panzer was a German tank implemented in 1934 and used throughout World War II with multiple variants. With the Treaty of Versailles in place, Germany was not allowed to manufacture armored vehicles, however, with the rise of Adolf Hitler, Germany began producing the Panzer I secretly. The Panzer I was a light tank designed for speed and mobility so that it could be used as a training vehicle, but also saw combat in the Spanish Civil War. Later on, the Panzer II entered production; this variant was also classed as a light tank but instead of carrying two machine guns, it was armed with a 20mm cannon. After the later versions of the Panzer were delayed, the Panzer II was utilized in the invasions of Poland, France, and the Soviet Union. However, the Panzer II proved ineffective against the Soviet tanks and was discontinued in 1942 in favor of the Panzer II and IV. The Panzer III was the first German tank to be classified as a medium tank and entered service in 1939. It was armed with a 37mm main gun and had a substantial increase in its thickness of armor. But it was quickly found again to be ineffective against the Soviet Union's T-34. This led to the development of the Panzer IV, which was used until the end of the war. It was armed with a 75mm gun but still was not as powerful as the T-34. The Panzer V was thus implemented near the end of the war as a counter to the T-34. It proved so successful that it was renamed to the Panther and a total of 5,000 were produced. At the end of the war, the Panzer VI or Tiger was introduced, it was armed with an 88mm gun, however, due to its complex design, only 1,350 units were produced. Overall, the Panzer was an extremely effective tank that although it remained widely ineffective against the Soviet tanks, it still provided a large threat to the allied forces.
